# ABOUT SPRINTIQ

This is a description of the SprintIQ product: what each page is for and how its parts
fit together. Use it to answer questions about how the system works, where something
lives, or what a feature does. It is a description of the product, not data about this
organisation: it contains no figures, and any number you quote still has to come from
the data you were given or a tool you called.

If a question is about a feature this guide does not mention, say you are not sure it
exists rather than describing one.

## WHAT SPRINTIQ IS

SprintIQ is an agile project management product with four machine-learning models built
in. Work is organised as organisation > projects > epics > user stories > tasks and
subtasks, planned in sprints. Every model prediction comes with a value, uncertainty
bounds, ranked drivers and a plain-language explanation, and can be accepted, overridden
or compared; that choice is recorded as feedback and in the audit trail.

## THE FOUR MODELS

- M1, estimation. Suggests story points for a story and predicts a sprint's velocity,
  then judges the sprint's commitment against that prediction. Shown in the story
  estimate panel on the project page and in sprint planning.
- M2, sprint risk. Three classifiers: over-budget risk (well calibrated), requirement-
  change risk (weak: misses more high-change sprints than it catches) and communication
  and collaboration risk (reads comments, blocked tasks and reopened work). A weighted
  overall figure combines them. The board keeps adjusting the score between assessments:
  blocked or reopened cards, tasks past their estimate, unanswered comments and logged
  requirement changes add points, and fixing them takes the points back.
- M3, resource allocation. Ranks team members for a task by fit, consistency, learning
  rate and current load. It scores fit for one item; it is not a performance review.
- M4, cost. Forecasts a sprint's cost against its budget, at planning time and again
  mid-sprint. Forecasts are typically 23-29% off, so the bounds matter.

## PAGES (left-hand navigation)

- Home. Dashboard of projects still in play, their health and risk at a glance.
- My Work. The tasks, stories and sprint assignments of the signed-in user, across all
  projects.
- Projects. Every project in the organisation with its explained health. Opening one
  shows the project overview and backlog, where a story can be selected and sized with
  the M1 estimate panel beside it. Each project also has a Sprints page, a Board and a
  Sprint risk page that bands every sprint in the project by overall risk.
- Roadmap. What the organisation intends to build, by project lane, and how far through
  it the work actually is.
- Goals & OKRs. Objectives with key results measured by numbers, not status colours.
- Timeline. Delivery against dates, built from epics, sprints and milestones.
  Requirement changes can be logged from here.
- Calendar. Everything already dated: sprint boundaries, milestones, due dates, targets.
- Board. A kanban board per project and sprint, with columns from the project's workflow
  states and their WIP limits. Each task card runs a work clock: it counts down against
  the estimate, pauses when the card is in Done and resumes if it is pulled back out
  (a reopen, which the risk model counts as rework).
- Backlog & Sprints. Pick a project, then plan and run sprints on one screen. A sprint's
  commitment freezes when it starts; work added afterwards is recorded as scope creep and
  work removed as scope reduction. Closing a sprint measures delivery against the frozen
  commitment and sends unfinished items to the backlog, the next sprint or a chosen one.
  Burndown comes from daily snapshots.
- Reports. Delivery analytics in tabs: Velocity, Cycle time, Lead time, Flow (cumulative
  flow) and People. Each tab loads its own report when opened.
- Risk Analysis. The M2 page for one sprint: Overview (four gauges, drivers, board
  findings, Assess now), Trend (risk over the sprint, day by day), Requirement changes
  (the register of logged changes) and Compare sprints (two to five side by side).
- Resource Allocation. The M3 page: allocation suggestions for tasks, the developers and
  their profiles, and sprint-level allocation.
- What-If Simulator. Try allocations before committing to them and see the effect.
- Sprint Cost. The M4 page: cost forecasts against budget and the cost trend.
- Copilot. A conversational assistant that can look things up across every project,
  sprint and work item and call all four models. It can propose changes (accept an
  estimate, assign a task, move a story to a sprint) as cards that a person must confirm;
  it never changes anything on its own. Conversations are saved and private.
- AI Assistant (AI insights). One project's velocity prediction, cost forecast, the three
  risk models, estimation coverage, and a chat about the current sprint.
- Automation. Not built yet: the page says so.
- Team. Who is carrying what: each person's load as a bar against 100%, with
  over-allocation shown rather than clipped.
- Documents. Specs, notes and decisions kept beside the work, with every edit kept.
- Notifications. Things that happened to the user or to work they own, plus delivery
  preferences.
- Settings (Administration). Team members and invitations, AI models (the model registry
  with usage and acceptance), Capabilities (switching each AI feature on or off for the
  workspace), Recycle bin, and the Audit trail for those allowed to read it.

## ACCESS AND PRIVACY

- What a person can see and do depends on their role's permissions. An assistant sees
  only what the signed-in user can see.
- An administrator can switch individual AI capabilities off in Settings > Capabilities;
  a switched-off model's panels disappear and the page says which are off.
- The chat assistants are the only parts of SprintIQ that send data to an external AI
  provider, and they are off unless an API key is configured. The four models run
  inside SprintIQ.
