Domain · research background

Sprint planning runs on four guesses.

How big is it? Who should take it? What will it cost? What could go wrong? Agile teams answer these with a planning-poker vote, whoever looks free, last quarter's spreadsheet, and whatever they find out on day nine. This page sets out what the research says about each guess, where it falls short, and how SprintIQ was built to close the gap.

01 · Literature survey

What's already known

Agile is now the default way software teams plan work, and sprint planning is where most of its decisions are made [1]. Each of those decisions has its own research thread. The four threads below are the ones SprintIQ builds on.

Sizing work: story points and velocity

Machine-learning estimation of story points from issue text started with classifiers over issue features [2] and moved to deep models: Deep-SE combined an LSTM with a recurrent highway network [3], and GPT2SP used a transformer [4]. A careful replication found that Deep-SE did not consistently beat simple baselines such as the median [5], so any new model has to be judged against those baselines. Team velocity has also been predicted from iteration history [6]. Almost all of this work returns a single number. Conformalised quantile regression gives a range with a coverage guarantee [7], but it hasn't been applied to sprint planning.

Staffing work: who should take it

Recommending a developer for a work item is well studied in bug triage, where models learn from who fixed similar reports before [8]. Those recommenders optimise for one goal: expertise. Sprint assignment has several goals that pull against each other: skill match, a fair workload, avoiding overload, and giving people room to grow. Multi-criteria methods such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process weigh competing goals explicitly [9], and ensembles such as stacking combine several predictors of performance [10].

Costing work: what will the sprint cost

Parametric models such as COCOMO estimate cost from size at the start of a project [11], and a systematic review found that expert judgement still dominates practice [12]. Earned value management compares actual spend with the plan as work progresses [13], but it extrapolates rather than learning what the rest of a sprint is likely to cost. Shapley values give a principled way to explain which factors moved a forecast [14].

Risk-checking work: what could go wrong

Issue-level risk, such as the chance an issue will be delayed, can be predicted from project data [15]. Gradient-boosted trees with native categorical handling, such as CatBoost, suit the mixed data a sprint produces [16]. Distilled language models such as DistilBERT make it practical to read risk signals from text [17]. Most risk models score a snapshot taken at planning time. They don't update as the board moves, and they don't say what to do next.

02 · Research gap

Tools record and report. They don't predict.

The shared gap

Agile tools record what happened and report on it. They don't predict what will happen, explain why, or say how sure they are, and the research models that do predict live outside the tools teams actually plan in.

M1 · Estimation

One number, no range

Story-point models return a single value with no calibrated range, so a team can't tell a confident 5 from a wild guess. Velocity models assume history from the same kind of project, so they break when a team starts work in a new domain.

M3 · Allocation

Skill match is not the whole job

Assignment recommenders optimise for expertise alone. Workload fairness, overload and growth are left out, and when several goals are combined the weights are set by hand rather than derived and tested.

M4 · Cost

Costed once, never revised

Sprint cost is estimated once at planning, from a formula or expert judgement. Forecasts aren't revised mid-sprint from the cost already incurred, and nobody can see which factors moved them.

M2 · Risk

Risk found on day nine

Risk is discussed at planning and discovered late. Models score a static snapshot, don't react to what happens on the board, and stop at a probability without recommending what to do.

03 · Research problem

The question we set out to answer

Main research problem

How can an Agile project management tool use machine learning to size, staff, cost and risk-check sprint work for Sri Lankan software teams, while showing how sure each prediction is and why, so that teams can trust it when they plan?

Each component answers one part of the main problem.
ComponentSpecific research problem
M1How can story points and next-sprint velocity be predicted with a range that keeps its promised coverage, including for a team working in a domain the model has never seen?
M3How can tasks be assigned so that skill match, workload balance, overload and growth are all weighed, with weights that are derived rather than guessed?
M4How can sprint cost be forecast at planning and re-forecast mid-sprint from the cost already incurred, with each forecast's drivers explained?
M2How can over-budget, requirement-change and communication risks be predicted, updated live from board events, and turned into recommended next steps?
04 · Research objectives

What we committed to build and prove

Main objective. To design, build and evaluate SprintIQ, an AI-driven Agile project management framework that improves productivity and project success in Sri Lanka's software industry, by attaching four explainable, uncertainty-aware models to the planning decisions they inform.

  1. M1Estimate story points and velocity with honest ranges

    Beat the median baseline on error, hit the 70% range-coverage target, and predict velocity for an unseen domain.

  2. M3Rank developers for each task fairly

    Blend predicted performance with skill, workload and growth, using AHP weights refined by simulation.

  3. M4Forecast sprint cost, then re-forecast mid-sprint

    Choose the planning method by evidence and explain every forecast with exact Shapley drivers.

  4. M2Predict sprint risks live and advise on them

    Score how bad and how likely each risk is, adjust from board events, and recommend steps.

  5. AILet people ask across all four models

    A Copilot that calls the models as tools, sees only what the user can see, and changes nothing without confirmation.

05 · Methodology

Design Science Research, one loop per model

SprintIQ is an artefact: something built to solve a real problem and then evaluated. That makes Design Science Research the natural method [21] [22]. We followed its six activities as a loop, once for the platform and once for each model.

How each activity was carried out in this project.
ActivityWhat we did
01 Identify the problemReviewed the literature and current sprint-planning practice; framed the four guesses and the shared gap.
02 Define objectivesSet measurable targets per model, each against a baseline: error vs the median estimate, 70% range coverage, success rate vs manual allocation, MAPE vs the remaining-estimate method, AUC for risk.
03 Design and developBuilt and tuned each model in Python, then the SprintIQ platform: a multi-tenant Spring Boot backend, a Next.js frontend and a FastAPI model service.
04 DemonstrateAttached each model to the decision it informs inside the product, with Accept, Override and Dismiss on every suggestion, and added the Copilot on top.
05 EvaluateHeld-out test sets, ten-sprint simulations and time-based holdouts, always beside a baseline. Every prediction the product makes is stored with its inputs for later study.
06 CommunicateProposal, progress presentations, research paper, final theses and this website.

Inside each model's loop

Data

Real sprint data with ground truth is rarely shared, so the models were trained on structured synthetic datasets. M1, for example, used 3,000 stories and 3,000 sprints across five domains. Validation on real organisational data continues.

Features

Text features from story titles and descriptions (TF-IDF), structural features such as dependencies and acceptance criteria, team capacity and history, developer skill profiles, and live board events.

Models

Gradient-boosted trees where the data is tabular: XGBoost [18], LightGBM [19] and CatBoost [16]; plus a stacking ensemble for developer performance and a fine-tuned DistilBERT for communication risk.

Range and reasons

Every model returns the same envelope: a value, its range, the drivers behind it and a one-line explanation. Ranges come from calibrated quantiles; drivers from SHAP [20] and exact Shapley values.

Evaluate

Each model is scored on held-out or simulated data beside the baseline it has to beat, never on its own. The results page lists every figure with that baseline.

Serve

Bundles load into a FastAPI service with /predict/* endpoints. The backend checks the contract at start-up, caches results, and records each prediction with its exact inputs.
